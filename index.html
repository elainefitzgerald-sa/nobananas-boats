No Bananas
On Boats

For three hundred years, sailors have refused to let a single banana aboard. They weren't being silly. They were being careful.

Scroll · the curse awaits
An old law of the sea

The oldest superstition afloat

Ask a lifelong fisherman to bring a banana on his boat and watch his face change. In marinas from the Carolinas to the Caribbean, the rule is unspoken and absolute: no bananas. Not the fruit, not banana bread, not Banana Boat sunscreen, not a shirt with Chiquita on the label.

It sounds absurd until you learn where it came from. The ban traces back to the 1700s, the golden age of trade when fortunes crossed the Atlantic in wooden hulls — and ships carrying bananas had a disturbing habit of never arriving. Over the years the fear hardened into one of the most widely held traditions in all of seafaring. Here's the case against the humble banana.

The evidence

Seven reasons to leave them on the dock

01

They sink ships (statistically)

In the 1700s, trading vessels lost at sea had an eerie tendency to be carrying bananas. To make the perishable fruit before it rotted, captains sailed fast and hard — straight through weather a wiser skipper would've waited out. The banana ships vanished; the legend was born.

02

They spoil everything nearby

Ripening bananas release ethylene gas, which makes all your other produce ripen and rot faster. On a long voyage with limited food, a crate of bananas could quietly spoil the entire larder before you reached port.

03

They smuggle snakes & spiders

Banana bunches were notorious for hiding venomous stowaways — spiders, snakes, and biting insects tucked in the stalks. A deadly bite mid-ocean, far from any doctor, and the crew blamed the fruit. Fairly.

04

They kill the fishing

The most stubborn belief of all: bananas aboard means you won't catch a thing. Charter captains swear by it to this day. One theory — fast banana-boats trolled too quickly for fish to bite, so "bananas" became shorthand for a dead day on the water.

05

They ferment into trouble

In the heat of a cargo hold, bananas fermented and released gases some believed were toxic — and in poorly ventilated holds, possibly were. Crews working below deck fell ill, and the fruit took the blame.

06

They rot before you profit

Pressure to deliver bananas while still yellow meant no stopping, no delays, no second chances. The fruit dictated the voyage — and a voyage ruled by panic is a voyage that ends badly.

07

They anger the sea itself

Centuries of bad luck calcified into pure superstition. Today the "why" barely matters. A banana on board is simply bad juju — and no one sensible tempts the ocean on a technicality.

From the mouths of mariners

They're not joking

"I've turned a man around at the dock and made him throw his lunch in the truck. Banana muffin. I don't care. It's not coming on my boat."

Modern sport-fishing culture takes the ban as seriously as any 18th-century deckhand. Entire tackle brands sell "no banana" flags and decals. Crews have been known to search coolers. Some captains extend the curse to anything banana-adjacent — Fruit of the Loom underwear, Banana Republic hats, even a certain brand of sunscreen have all been tossed ashore.

Is it rational? Mostly not. Is it worth the risk on the one day the fish finally show up? Absolutely not.

The ruling
Verdict: Leave the Bananas

You don't have to believe in curses. You just have to respect three centuries of sailors who all independently decided the same thing. Here's the deckhand's code:

  • No bananas. No banana bread, chips, or muffins.
  • Check the sunscreen — Banana Boat stays in the car.
  • No banana-brand clothing. The sea reads labels.
  • If in doubt, it goes on the dock. Every time.
  • When the fish aren't biting, search the cooler.
